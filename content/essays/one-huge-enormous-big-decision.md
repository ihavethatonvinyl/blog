The year was 2014, and I had just decided to take on one of the biggest choices of my life that had nothing to do with buying a record (which has always come rather easy to me). I had already proposed; my fiancée and I were now prepping guest lists, invitations, and such. I felt like the heavy mental lifting was over, and we would work out the rest together. But then, she raised one question that only I could answer: would our household, our hopefully soon-to-be family, stay interfaith? To resolve this, I did another thing that came remarkably easy to me: spend way too much time reading, researching, and overthinking. My conclusion? I decided that I wanted to be a full participant and partner in all aspects of our life, but - more than that - I wanted to be an equal source of knowledge and support for our (then still hypothetical) children. It was easy to feel not just right, but righteous, like I just knew the truly correct thing to do and could follow it all the way down. So I declared my intent, started meeting with a rabbi, and joined a course that would officially end with conversion.

It turned out making it happen, though, was not nearly so easy. I was almost immediately surrounded by new ideas, new customs, a new language, and was trying to master it all day by day with a wedding fast approaching. Doubts crept in, not about whether to keep going with this path, but what was going to happen now that I had? How could I hope to keep up with those that had been born into this? How much of myself would I lose on the way? On a more immediate level, I needed help understanding practical things like just how to spell, recite, or prepare for the approaching holidays, things I was hesitant to admit I hadn’t gotten down yet. I found myself feeling like, on my fiancėe’s side of the family, I was being watched nervously. Meanwhile, on my side, I was suddenly an authority expected to walk them through these new practices. Caught in between novice and expert, between the comfortable and the vital, I felt like I had no true confidant who could make things stop spinning and help me feel more settled.

But then, a pat on the back came from a new voice within a totally familiar arena. For several years running, I’d made a tradition of sharing long holiday music mixes with friends. It felt only natural to soft-launch this new part of my life by injecting more Hanukkah this time around. While researching, I came across The LeeVees, who had never been on my radar before, and who had put out just one LP (*Hanukkah Rocks*). Strangely enough, though, a glance at their site showed their members included Dave Schneider from The Zambonis, omnipresent every hockey season in my college dorm, and Adam Gardner from Guster, one of the bands I’d treasured most as far back as I could remember! That was enough for me to put on the album, hoping it would at least give me some catchy candidates to include in that year’s missive. Instead, what came rushing through the speakers did so much more than that.

Music has always been able to reach me in a direct way other things couldn’t (I wrote my college application essay about what “Everybody Hurts” said to me at a particularly hard moment in life). In this same fashion, Hanukkah Rocks reached out with a primer of lessons for my first Hanukkah season as a not quite finalized Jew. First and foremost, what was waiting ahead was something that was supposed to be FUN. The album is driven by the kind of catchy, melodic rock that was immediately familiar from my Guster fandom, but also touches on music hall and a ripping Ramones homage (“Gelt Melts,” all about the perils of losing hard-won chocolate coins to your own body heat). Dave’s mom even turns up for a phone conversation skit, complete with a classic guilt trip at the end. I couldn’t help but smile riding through these wacky left turns.

Then I tuned in more to the details of what was being said; the opening track (“Latke Clan”) is a literal invitation to “join our…clan” and evoked the magic of rushing home from school for a holiday celebration. Ok, I told myself, settling into the nostalgia, this is something to look forward to rather than dread. Next up, “Apple Sauce vs Sour Cream” points out the “enormous, big decision” hanging over the holiday is really just what you put on your potato pancakes, while “Goyim Friends” states plainly how Hanukkah isn’t Jewish Christmas, just close in seasonal proximity. More tube socks and takeout Chinese food, less turkey carvings and gigantic gifts. While Hanukkah will always suffer in that comparison, we’re told, it’s ok to put on a brave face and “march on.” Finally, something sunk in for me that it can take time to understand from the outside - Hanukkah isn’t that big and hairy a deal in the Jewish calendar anyway! It may sound silly, but hearing the stakes were not actually so high as I’d made them in my head was a relief.

My ears perked up along the way at talk of having to spend time with weird uncles from out of town, answering awkward questions, and the actual reality behind big family gatherings. On top of the humor of it all, it made real for me that not only could I do this, I had been doing it even if in a different context. Able to relax a bit, I showed up at my in-laws’ place not playing wallflower, but participating in the rituals and being warmly received. 

Meanwhile, penultimate song “Nun Gimmel Heh Shin” left me with a ringing memory of the words symbolized while playing dreidel in time for my first Synagogue holiday gathering. I was able to keep up with each spin, and later even walked my side of the family through how to do the same. Small things, surely, but enough for me to feel more comfortable in my own skin in those moments. Ever since, *Hanukkah Rocks* has embodied that comfort and remained a companion in the dark nights of winter.

Cut to almost ten years later, and it had done that job well for a long time. Then the job description, along with our whole world, changed when we finally welcomed a little one in 2024 after quite a long road. I had grown accustomed to celebrating and practicing our holiday rituals, but now having to teach and explain them? Yes, it’s why I went through all this in the first place, but that fantasy becoming real felt overwhelming all over again. As fate would have it, our kid’s first time walking around for Hanukkah was also the 20th anniversary of Hanukkah Rocks. The band marked it with a loving, latke-styled vinyl repress, one I brought right into our home. Now I could display, point to, and share it with our new member each year as they grew, a shared base of knowledge in which I could ground any lessons.

Of course, like with most everything in the news each day, I wondered just how I’m supposed to help her understand living in a post-Tree Of Life, post-2016 Election world. Thankfully, Adam and Dave read my mind again. Included on the reissue is a new song, “The Penny,” looking back at an incident of harassment Dave had gone through in his own life. The echoes of that day clearly haven’t disappeared for him, but now were being amplified more widely in a way that he must have felt compelled to address:

*All the kids that used to be so mean/Shouting at their tv screens* 

*They keep getting prouder/And they’re getting louder*

Like the rest of the album did all those years past, playing “The Penny” over and over helped solidify something in me: that our path forward isn’t about shrinking away. It’s about my modeling for my daughter how we best lean in, just as The LeeVees had helped me do in taking my first steps into our family’s new ways. It’s about taking that attitude of “march on” from Hanukkah and carrying it all year round:

*Stand up straight/Hold your head up high*

*It’s ok if you need to cry*

*This world’s a crazy place*

*Fill it up with love/Let love replace the hate*”

A new tradition of what we play each year in December - along with life lessons that will last forever - all from a band that was explicitly intended as a one-off holiday novelty at first. Only you can decide if you think that’s on par with oil burning for eight times as long as it should’ve, but to this head and heart it’s surely a mitzvah that will light our home for years to come.
