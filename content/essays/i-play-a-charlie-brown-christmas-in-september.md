The first morning that feels like it might turn cold, I get up and put on *A Charlie Brown Christmas*. Usually this is in September. I know how that sounds, so I'll say up front that I'm not doing it as a joke.

It's closer to checking the weather. I notice the floor is cold through my socks, or that the light is coming in lower than it did last week, and I think, okay, here we go, and I walk over to the shelf. I drop the needle on "Christmas Time Is Here" and for the next few minutes the apartment feels like it's waiting for December. The radiator isn't even on yet. I'm probably still in shorts.

The record is the Vince Guaraldi Trio's *A Charlie Brown Christmas*, which came out in 1965 alongside the TV special. I own three copies. That's two more than anyone needs, and since the copies are the embarrassing part, I'll go through them.

The first is the 2012 Fantasy picture disc with the snowflake on it. I bought it first, on purpose, because I wanted the nice one. It looks great. I've played it a few times and it sounds fine.

The second is a green vinyl reissue, and I did not need it. I was flipping through a dump bin in the middle of summer and there it was for twelve dollars. A Christmas record sitting in a bin in July made me feel bad for it, like seeing a dog left in a parked car. I already owned the album, so I knew this wasn't a real reason to spend twelve dollars. I spent it anyway.

The third I found at a church rummage sale, also in July, in a box on the floor near some Mantovani. It's an original stereo pressing and it's in rough shape. The jacket is worn soft at the corners and the spine has split. On the paper sleeve inside, somebody wrote their name in ballpoint. I paid a few dollars. I don't know who they are, and I've looked at that handwriting more than I'd like to admit.

This is the one I play. The other two stay in their jackets on the shelf. I can't fully explain it. There's surface noise before the music starts, a soft static, and I've gotten to where I wait for it. The picture disc is quieter and I like it less. I think I bought the nice copies to prove I took the record seriously, and then the copy I actually listen to turned out to be the one somebody else had already been listening to.

As for why this record, and not some other one, I'm not a musician, so all I can tell you is what the piano does to me. Guaraldi never sounds like he's in a hurry. The notes come out a little dry and a little round, and there's a lot of room between them. Even the sad songs don't feel heavy. They feel like someone walking home slowly, who doesn't mind that it's dark out.

"Christmas Time Is Here" is one of the sad ones, and the kids singing it are not polished. Some of them come in slightly behind the others. When I was little I assumed they were just bad at it. Now it's the part I'd miss most. A lot of Christmas music is trying to talk you into being happy, and this doesn't try. It sounds like a room of children doing what they were asked to do, with a piano underneath them that quietly knows how the year went.

From what I can tell, kids today still sit through the special the way I did, waiting out the talking and the slow parts to get to the music. I think that matters more than it seems. It's rare for anything made for children to ask them to sit still and be a little sad for a while, and they do it.

The record also just keeps getting pressed. Every few years there's another color or another anniversary edition, and it never has the feel of a cash-in. Most holiday albums are gone by January, like the wrapping paper. This one gets reissued and sits with the jazz records, and I've contributed to that myself, twice, once for no reason.

I could make this an essay about collecting and what it says about a person that he keeps the good copies sealed up and wears out the bad one. Maybe it says something. I think it mostly says I wanted to own the record and then found out I wanted to hear it, which is different, and it took me three purchases to notice.

When the side ends I lift the needle, slide the record back into its beat-up jacket, and go make coffee. It's still September. There's a whole stretch of fall between me and anything that looks like Christmas, work and deadlines and everything else, and none of that has changed. But the season has started, at least in my apartment.

I'll play it again in November, and again in December. The first time is the one I'm really after, though. I don't know whether the person who wrote their name on the sleeve would have minded me starting this early. I assume not.
